1.  Introduction

Research  into  stakeholder  perceptions  of  conventional  aquaculture  has  focused  upon  issues  such  as  risk,
impact  on  other  users  of  marine  space,  and  the  factors  inﬂuencing  consumers.  However,  some  of  these
issues  may  become  redundant  with  new  aquaculture  practices  such  as  Integrated  Multi-trophic
Aquaculture  (IMTA).  For  practices  such  as  IMTA  to  realise  their  potential  they  must  be  socially  acceptable
and  this  may  depend  on  satisfying  key  stakeholders.  This  study  used  in-depth  interviews  to  identify
potential  concerns  of  stakeholders  as  well  as  perceived  beneﬁts  in  relation  to  the  development  of  IMTA  to
a  commercial  level  of  production.  A  total  of  44  interviews  were  conducted  across  12  stakeholder  groups
in  six  countries:  Cyprus,
Italy,  Norway  and  Scotland.  Levels  of  awareness  and
understanding  of  IMTA  were  mixed  across  stakeholder  groups  and  across  countries.  Concerns  were
raised  including:  spatial  location  issues,  food  safety  and  disease.  Perceived  beneﬁts  of  IMTA  included:
waste  utilisation,  minimisation  of  impacts  to  the  benthos  and  the  ﬁltering  of  sea  lice  larvae.  Also
recognised  as  important  was  the  creation  of  new  income  streams.  The  results  showed  that  some  issues/
beneﬁts  were  more  important  in  certain  countries.  Risks  to  the  environment,  risks  arising  from
governance  and  risks  to  the  industry  itself  were  raised,  but  stakeholders  felt  that  these  sources  of  risk
could  be  addressed  by  research  studies,  education  and  changes  to  legislation.  Stakeholders  also  believed
that  IMTA  could  contribute  to  improving  the  image  of  the  aquaculture  industry.  For  IMTA  to  successfully
move  forward  from  pilot  scale  to  commercial  scale  development,  it  is  imperative  that  the  issues  raised  in
this  study  form  priorities  for  reform  and  action.

2.  Materials  and  methods

2.1.  Data  collection:  in-depth  interviews

In-depth  interviews  were  selected  as  a  suitable  method  of
accessing  detailed  qualitative  information  about  opinions  and
attitudes.  Research  interviews  are  used  for  four  reasons:  (i)  to  ﬁll  a
gap  in  knowledge  that  other  methods  are  unable  to  bridge,  (ii)  to
investigate  complex  behaviours  and  motivations,  (iii)  to  collect  a
diversity  of  meaning,  opinions  and  experience,  and  (iv)  to  use  a
method  which  shows  respect  for  and  empowers  those  people  who
provide  the  data  (Hay,  2005).  Despite  such  disadvantages  as  time
and  cost  (amongst  others),  there  are  advantages  to  using  the  in-
depth  interview  method  such  as  being  able  to  untangle  complex
topics  whilst  also  combining  structure  with  ﬂexibility.

Interviews  were  undertaken  in  each  country  with  a  selection  of
stakeholders  identiﬁed  in  an  earlier  study  using  a  stakeholder
analysis  technique.  An  attempt  was  made  to  obtain  a  wide
coverage  of  stakeholder  groups,  although  due  to  difﬁculties  in
contacting  stakeholders  this  was  not  always  possible  within  each
national  case  study.  In  addition,  stakeholder  groups  were  not

In-depth  interviews  were  carried  out  using  a  topic  guide.  The
guide  was  split  into  four  sections  (one  of  which  had  two  sub-
sections).  The  ﬁrst  section  identiﬁed  the  awareness  and  under-
standing  of  the  stakeholder  regarding  IMTA;  where  the  stakeholder
had  no  awareness  of  IMTA  the  interviewer  would  either  explain  or
show  an  explanatory  video.  Section  two  investigated  the  stakehold-
er  perceptions  of  IMTA  (in  terms  of  positives  and  negatives).  Section
three  focused  upon  risks  to  development  of  the  industry  and  ways  in
which  to  mitigate  this  risk.  The  ﬁnal  section  queried  industry  image
and  the  impacts  that  IMTA  would  have  upon  this  image.

Informed  consent  was  obtained  from  the  participants  and
interviews  were  recorded  using  an  audio  recorder.  Field  notes
regarding  time,  location  and  general  impressions  were  also  written
after  the  interview  by  a  number  of  the  project  partners.

4.  Discussion

There  are  limitations  to  using  a  face-to-face  interview  method
including  the  potential  for  an
interviewer  to  unintentionally
inﬂuence  results  through  verbal  and  non-verbal  cues.  Normally,  to
reduce  this  bias,  only  one  interviewer  would  be  used.  In  this  study
the  use  of  a  single  interviewer  was  not  possible  due  to  language
barriers  and  time  constraints.  Furthermore,  unintentional  bias  was
difﬁcult  to  identify  in  this  study  as  interviews  were  not  transcribed
verbatim  and  translated  to  English  in  this  format.  A  further
limitation  may  relate  to  the  lack  of  spread  of  interviews  between
stakeholder  groups  in  each  of  the  countries  and  the  experience  of
the  interviewees  in  social  science  delivery.  In  some  stakeholder
groups  only  one  or  two  interviews  were  conducted.  This  makes  it
more  difﬁcult  to  investigate  similarities  and  differences  within  the
group  and  across  the  population.  Due  to  the  low  number  of
interviews  undertaken  within  some  stakeholder  groups,  differ-
ences  between  stakeholder  groups  will  not  be  discussed.  Further-
more,  as  already  noted,  different

countries;  however,  it  is  also  important  to  note  that  this  reﬂects
cultural  and  economic  differences  between  countries.

beneﬁts  to  the  environment  such  as  waste  utilisation  and  economic

issues,  disease  outbreak

related  to  containment  and  governance  issues,  but  stakeholders  felt

education  and  changes  to  legislation.  Currently,  the  legislative
framework  across  European  partner  countries  is  broadly  similar  but
certainly  lacking  in  sufﬁcient  clarity  with  regards  to  IMTA  for  a

primarily  on  its  dependence  on  a  variety  of  different  regulations  and
policies  at  European  and  national  levels  (Alexander  et  al.,  2015).
Overall  it  was  felt  that  IMTA  would  contribute  to  improving  the
image  of  the  aquaculture  industry  but  many  issues  such  as  trophic
interactions  would  need  to  be  explored  and  clariﬁed  with  decision
makers,  the  seafood  industry  and  the  general  public.

More  respondents  were  aware  of  IMTA  than  unaware,  contra-
dicting  the  study  undertaken  in  Canada  which  found  that  78%  of
focus  group  participants  had  never  heard  of  the  term  IMTA
(Barrington  et  al.,  2010),  although  the  majority  of  respondents  in
that  Canadian  study  did  not  work  within  the  industry.  It  is
unsurprising  that,  in  the  European  investigation  presented  in  this
paper,  those  with  higher  levels  of  awareness  were  stakeholders
involved  directly  in  the  aquaculture  industry,  most  likely  arising
from  a  general  interest  in  advances  in  aquaculture  husbandry,
technology,  etc.  Regarding  the  higher  awareness  of
