General overview

New Zealand Aquaculture industry

Aquaculture in New Zealand commenced in the early 1960s with the production of the
indigenous rock oyster Saccostrea commercialis on the North Island (FAO 2007). The
rock oyster was replaced by the non-indigenous Paciﬁc oyster (Crassostrea gigas) in the
1970s and this species subsequently became the focus of oyster farming. At the same
time, following the collapse of the dredge mussel ﬁsheries, marine farmers began culturing
the indigenous green-lipped mussel (Perna canaliculus) on ropes. Mussel farming is now
the cornerstone of the New Zealand aquaculture industry, marketed under the trademark
of Greenshell™ mussels (Dawber 2004).

Although New Zealand has no indigenous salmon species, several species were introduced
in the early 1900s to start a commercial rod ﬁshing and canning industry. Chinook (or King)
salmon (Oncorhynchus tschawytscha) populations became established on the South Island
(Haworth 2010) and the ﬁrst commercial farms were established between 1976 and 1983.
The salmon industry has grown into one of the largest producers of farmed Chinook
salmon in the world (FAO 2007). In 2019 the New Zealand aquaculture industry harvested
97,462 t of Greenshell™ mussels, 14,180 t of Chinook salmon and 1,805 t of Paciﬁc oysters
with a total sales value of NZ$673 million (US$436 million) (Table 1).

New Zealand has 15,000 km of coastline, making it one of the longest in the world.
However, there are currently only around 20,000 hectares of water space allocated for
marine farming. Almost all the current commercial production comes from near-shore
areas which account for 40% of the total allocation. These areas include Northland har-
bours, the Coromandel Peninsula, Tasman and Golden Bays, the Marlborough Sounds,
Banks Peninsula and Stewart Island (Figure 1). The remaining area comprises three
large oﬀshore sites (47%) where mooring technology together with small-scale production
is being tested.

Although a relatively small industry in New Zealand, aquaculture is important econ-
omically as it provides rural employment and business opportunities. The industry has
grown to a signiﬁcant economic activity over the last 40 years generating important

Once collected, mussel seed are transferred to on-growing sites where they take between
15 and 18 months to grow to a harvestable size of 90 to 100 mm. Mussel on-growing is
undertaken using a longline system, usually in shallow coastal waters of 15–30 m depth.
There are approximately 645 mussel farms consents in New Zealand, with a typical
near-shore 3 ha mussel farm producing around 120 t of mussels at harvest. Farmers do
not own their farm sites but are granted a coastal permit by regional councils to use the
water space.

In recent years the mussel industry has become highly consolidated, particularly with
regard to processing and marketing. While many independent farms still exist, these gen-
erally grow mussels under contract to the larger processing companies (Stimpson and
Company 2007).

The production of Chinook salmon (Oncorhynchus tschawytscha) in New Zealand is
largely a historical accident. In the early 1900s Chinook, Atlantic and Sockeye salmon
were introduced to New Zealand. The chinook populations became established and
only small remnant populations of Atlantic and Sockeye salmon remained. In the early
1980s initial attempts to establish salmon farms in New Zealand, therefore, focused on
the more widely available Chinook salmon (Camara and Symonds 2014). Salmon
farming in New Zealand occurs at both freshwater and marine sites. Marine production
accounts for 86% of the 12,000 t annual harvest, with two companies (New Zealand
King Salmon and Sanford) accounting for 97% of this production.

Over half of all salmon production occurs within sea-pens at sites within the Marlbor-
ough Sounds. In recent years a number of these sites have recorded higher mortalities due
to increased water temperatures and low water ﬂows. Summer water temperatures within
parts of the Sounds approach the upper thermal limit for Chinook salmon and the indus-
try has relocated a number of farms to new sites that are closer to the open coast and have
higher ﬂow rates.

Chinook salmon is generally regarded as a premium salmon species in terms of taste
and nutritional quality, possessing a higher fat and Omega-3 oil content, larger ﬁllet
size and better texture characteristics than Atlantic Salmon. Consequently, New
Zealand salmon farmers have managed to develop a niche market and premium price
for their product and compete successfully with Atlantic Salmon.

As with mussels, Paciﬁc oyster farmers in New Zealand are reliant on wild seed collec-
tion, although more recently an increasing proportion are using hatchery spat (Camara
and Symonds 2014). The industry primarly uses an intertidal rack culture system that
on-grows oyster seed that naturally settles onto limed sticks. However, there is increasing
use of alternative grow-out systems, including plastic trays and a hanging longline subtidal
system developed in Australia (FAO 2007). Consequently, demand has increased for
hatchery produced seed for stocking these systems.

The appearance of the Ostreid herpesvirus-1 (OsHV-1) in 2010/11 caused signiﬁcant
mortality in farmed oyster stocks and reduced the scale of the industry. However,
farmers are learning to manage the disease and production levels are beginning to
return to the pre-2010 levels. The potential to selectively breed for resilience among
other phenotypic traits of interest has also been possible thanks to the development of
hatchery production for this species in New Zealand (Camara et al. 2017).

Maintaining water quality is a signiﬁcant concern for oyster farmers. New Zealand’s
clean water makes it possible for oysters to be eaten raw without depuration, which is a
signiﬁcant marketing advantage over international competitors.

Other Species

In common with many countries, a wide range of species have been investigated for their
aquaculture potential in New Zealand (Alfaro et al. 2014). Currently, research organis-
ations are focusing on improved understanding of aquaculture and environmental inter-
actions and development of higher value species that provide opportunities to deliver
improved export revenues from existing marine farm space.

The black foot abalone (Haliotis iris) (locally known as pāua) is indigenous to New
Zealand and has received signiﬁcant attention with regard to its culture for meat and
pearls (Camara and Symonds 2014). Despite culture techniques being established in the
1980s, due to its slow growth rates there is currently little commercial production of
abalone (120 t) with one large land-based farm and hatchery (Moana Blue Pāua) account-
ing for much of this production.

The commercial development of the indigenous Bluﬀ oyster (Tiostrea chilensis) has
received considerable attention since the 1980s (Jeﬀs 1999), with commercial production
being established around 2012. Development has subsequently been halted by the discov-
ery of the haemocyte parasite Bonamia ostreae in 2015 which prevented movement of
stock and led to all the farms being disestablished in order to protect indigenous wild
stocks.

Trials on seaweed cultivation started in the late 1980s, but there has been little commer-
cial interest on seaweed cultivation in New Zealand to date, with few projects making it
beyond research-scale cultivation (Zemke-White et al. 1999). The seaweeds that have
been successfully cultured include Gigartina spp., Pyropia spp. (formerly Porphyra), Gra-
cilaria (Gracilaria chilensis), small agar weed (Pterocladia capillacea), agar weed

Regulations, biosecurity and sanitation

Sector development is driven by The New Zealand Aquaculture Strategy (2019) which
outlines the industry’s vision and goals to achieve NZ$3 billion in annual sales by 2035.
Aquaculture New Zealand (AQNZ) is the organisation tasked with the implementation
of
the Strategy through legislative reform advocacy, research, environmental and
market development strategies. Aquaculture legislation passed in 2011 has modiﬁed exist-
ing laws and provided a regulatory framework that will better enable access to marine
space for aquaculture development.

Marine aquaculture is managed under New Zealand’s Resource Management Act 1991
(RMA) which promotes sustainable management of natural resources. Under the RMA
regional councils are responsible for planning and managing aquaculture in their
coastal area and any new marine farm must have a resource consent from the regional
council. To obtain a consent, farms must be able to demonstrate that they will not have
adverse eﬀects on other stakeholders. Subsequent to obtaining a resource consent,
farmers must also undergo an assessment of their potential impacts on commercial
ﬁsheries (undue adverse eﬀects test) (MPI 2013) before they can be granted a resource
consent from regional councils.

Prior to 2011 farmers could apply to set up new farms only in aquaculture management
areas (AMAs) established by councils. AMAs were introduced as a management tool in
2004 but due to the complications of setting up an AMA, no new farms were approved
under that regime. The 2011 legislative changes simpliﬁed the approval process by remov-
ing the need for AMAs, but this has not led to a signiﬁcant increase in the number of
farms.

In 2017 the Ministry of Primary Industries (MPI), in partnership with the Ministry for
the Environment and the Department of Conservation, proposed the development of a
National Environmental Standard for marine aquaculture (NES) under the RMA. The
proposed NES for marine aquaculture seeks to provide a more eﬃcient and certain
consent process for managing existing marine farms within environmental limits and to
implement a nationally consistent
framework for biosecurity management on all
marine farms. The NES is due to be enacted by 2020.

Due to its geographic isolation, New Zealand has relatively few pests or diseases associ-
ated with either its terrestrial or marine farming activities. The Government invests signiﬁ-
cantly in biosecurity to protect primary industries such as aquaculture from pest incursions
and to manage any incursions that do occur (Castinel et al. 2014, 2019; MPI 2016). Examples
of this can be seen from the impact of, and response to, the arrival of the Oyster Herpes
Virus in 2010/11 and the Bonamia ostreae parasite in 2015. These caused signiﬁcant mor-
talities and resulted in signiﬁcant losses for the industry. Other pests, such as Styela clava (a
non-indigenous sea squirt) and Sabella spallanzanii (the non-indigenous Mediterranean fan
worm) do not directly cause mortalities but add signiﬁcantly to the management costs for
marine farms and may aﬀect crop yield (Soliman and Inglis 2018).

As an export-focused industry, producers are reliant on New Zealand’s reputation for
safe and high-quality seafood. To protect consumers, there are stringent shellﬁsh quality
assurance programmes for all areas growing molluscan shellﬁsh. Farmers must have
shellﬁsh sanitation management plans in place to meet the requirements of these pro-

IMTA research and development

A range of supporting research and development services exist in New Zealand to under-
pin aquaculture growth. Crown Research Institutes (CRIs), universities and private sector
researchers undertake applied research for aquaculture production, processing technol-
ogies and the sustainability of aquaculture in coastal marine environments. This research
is funded mainly by Government research grants, as well as relevant ministries (e.g. MPI
and the Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment (MBIE)), regional councils and
industry.

Emergence

In 2001, the Environment and Fisheries ministries imposed a moratorium on the further
development of sea space for aquaculture to avoid proposed reforms being made ineﬀec-
tive by a rush of pre-emptive applications. Once these reforms were in place the morator-
ium was lifted in 2004 but the new processes to obtain farm space proved onerous and
expensive, and no new marine farming areas were designated under the new legislation.
This raised the question of how to increase production per unit area and IMTA was ident-
iﬁed through the Government’s International Investment Opportunity Fund (IIOF) to
have potential to contribute toward more eﬀective environmental and economic use of
existing sea space. An IMTA research project started in 2006 as a collaboration between
the National Institute of Water & Atmospheric Research Ltd (NIWA), the Yellow Sea
Fisheries Research Institute (YSFR) in China and the salmon and mussel industries
(Stenton-Dozey 2007). The main aim of this project was to investigate opportunities for
marine farmers to maximise returns from their existing sites through diversiﬁcation
into species that can occupy the water space within the farm lease without competing
for resources with the mussels or salmon that are already cultured on the sites. This
project focused on characterising the local hydrodynamics (Plew 2011a, 2011b), the
pelagic and benthic footprints around a mussel and salmon farm, and then assessing

Sea cucumbers

As deposit-feeders, sea cucumbers have been regular candidates for integration into multi-
trophic farming systems around the world (Zamora et al. 2018). In New Zealand, A. mollis
is the only commercially valuable species of sea cucumber which is subject to a small but
growing ﬁshery and has attracted considerable interest from the aquaculture sector
(Stenton-Dozey and Heath 2009; Zamora and Jeﬀs 2013; Heath et al. 2015; MPI 2018).
Wild-caught New Zealand sea cucumber is consumed in local Asian restaurants and
exported in its dried form (mostly the golden brown and dark brown colour morphs
are selected) where it can reach values ∼US$ 300–600 per kg depending on presentation
format. However, there has been increasing interest in the commercial culture and sea-
based on-growing of A. mollis.

Mussels

The dependence of the New Zealand aquaculture industry on the mussel Perna canaliculus
has prompted studies on the biology and ecophysiology of this species using DEB
(Dynamic Energy Budget) modelling (Ren and Ross 2001, 2005; Ren and Schiel 2008).
The models respond adequately to temperature and seasonal food supply and can simulate
trajectories of mussel growth and condition in farming ecosystems. Ren et al. (2010) have
extended this research by coupling the pelagic ecosystem, mussel energetics, population
dynamics and hydrodynamics to allow full dynamic predictions of the eﬀect of shellﬁsh
density on ecosystems.

DEB models have been used in IMTA research to assess the energetics of P. canaliculus
on a diet of salmon waste. In the vicinity of a 1,200 t salmon farm, mussel growth (ﬂesh
weight) was on average 12% higher within 50–100 m of the farm compared to control sites
over a period of one year (Stenton-Dozey, pers. obs.). Although it has been shown that the
assimilation eﬃciency of salmon particulates by mussels is low (Reid et al. 2010), the high
protein and glycogen content of salmon waste over the usual mussel diet of phytoplankton
and detritus, boosts ﬂesh growth and elevates the omega-3 fatty acid content (Lander et al.
2004).

Blue mussels (Mytilus galloprovincialis) recruit onto the mussel lines of P. canaliculus
and are considered a pest biofouler by industry (Atalah et al. 2017; Forrest and Atalah
2017). Despite large inter-annual and spatial variability in recruitment patterns onto
mussel farms, there has been an upward trend in the abundance of M. galloprovincialis
over the last 20 years (Atalah et al. 2017). Until recently, blue mussels were discarded
at harvest but now an emerging enterprise is focused on turning this by-catch into a valu-
able stream of new products, reducing waste and creating jobs. The blue mussel ﬂesh is
broken down into a homogenous solution for use as an ingredient for protein-rich aqua-
culture feed, for farmed ﬁsh such as salmon, shrimp and prawn. Potential also lies in using
blue mussels to create a dry, water-soluble peptide for nutraceuticals and nutritional
supplements.

Seaweeds

As part of the NIWA-IMTA research project, spore settlement and on-growing techniques
were developed for culture of the brown seaweed Ecklonia radiata in recognition that sea-
weeds can play a bioremedial role in coculture by absorbing a proportion of dissolved
inorganic nitrogen released by ﬁnﬁsh (Ren et al. 2012; Broch et al. 2013). These seaweeds
were on-grown with limited success in close proximity to mussel and salmon farms (Neill
et al. 2008). In the western Marlborough Sounds, E. radiata was suspended on vertical

The non-indigenous kelp Undaria pinnatiﬁda grows profusely on mussel farms and a
recent change in the biosecurity status of this species means that there is now potential to
both harvest and culture U. pinnatiﬁda on marine farms in selected areas (MAF 2010;
South et al. 2017) (Figure 4). In the early 1990s, a research project investigated the feasi-
bility of commercial U. pinnatiﬁda cultivation. Gametophyte cultures preserved the
characteristics of the originally introduced populations and provided material from
which large harvestable sporophytes were grown. Trial crops were grown near Wellington
and in the Marlborough Sounds but met with varied success, and later research focused on
identifying the most favourable genetic strains and environmental conditions for commer-
cial cultivation (Gibbs and Hay 1998; Gibbs 2000).

A recent resurgence of interest in U. pinnatiﬁda (Mandrekar 2018) has promoted col-
laboration between the Marine Farming Association (MFA 2019) and the Marlborough
District Council to facilitate dialogue between industry and researchers to advance
culture and marketing. The seaweed is now seasonally harvested oﬀ mussel lines with
100 t collected in 2018. Most of this product is made into organic plant care products
for viticulture, kiwifruit industries and pastoral farming. The demand is increasing with
consumers and primary producers seeking cleaner alternatives to chemical products as
concern over fertiliser use and nitrogen leaching are growing.

cesses the seaweed as a fresh product for local restaurants and the food service sector in
New Zealand and Australia. Recently, a product and capability development pilot

Opportunities and constraints to IMTA uptake

There are many recognised opportunities to grow the aquaculture sector in New Zealand,
inclusive of IMTA. Research and commercial interest are growing in expanding high-value
bioactive extraction from salmon, mussels and seaweeds for the nutraceutical industry.
Oﬀshore aquaculture space has been allocated for mussel farming, totalling 9,300 ha,

some of which has been developed to trial mooring infrastructure. A land-based ﬁsh
hatchery and nursey is high on the agenda for kingﬁsh and hāpuku with space available
for outgrowing in the North Island and salmon farmers are looking to move operations
to open ocean sites. The constraints on these opportunities are uncertainty around regu-
latory settings, spat/smolt capacity, operational costs and research and investment
funding.

Changing legislation and regulations

The new aquaculture legislation introduced in 2011 removes the need for aquaculture
developments to occur within pre-designated zones, allowing applications to be treated
on a case-by-case basis. These changes have ignited enthusiasm in the industry and
open the door to mixed-species farms and inter alia the possibility of IMTA development.
However, long-term tenure of sea space is still an issue as water space cannot be privatised
and present consents only run for approximately 15–20 years.

The use of ‘public’ space for commercial marine farms is hotly debated in New Zealand,
and new farm developments often receive objections from recreational boat users,
ﬁshermen, adjacent landowners and conservationists. Currently, the industry occupies
only 0.03% of inshore space and gaining access to more space will require the industry
to gain a stronger social license to operate by demonstrating that it is sustainable in the
long term and that visual impact issues have been addressed.

The use of seabed space for aquaculture is also a contentious issue. Current legislation
provides for wild ﬁshing industry ownership of all stock on the seabed. This precludes
seabed culture or ranching of, for example, sea cucumbers, sea urchins or scallops.
Besides commercial potential, these species provide potential biomitigative services for
marine farming. Revised legislative and/or local regional council planning adjustments
would be required to facilitate the use of the seabed in allocated aquaculture space and
to recognise potential environmental bioremediation from the synergistic interactions
between co-cultured species.

It is necessary to recognise and understand that IMTA systems are not restricted by
absolute distances (i.e. the species being integrated don’t necessarily have to be next to
each other on the same farm). The scale that deﬁnes integration depends on interactions
between the local topography, hydrodynamics, nutrient cycles/ﬂows and ecological func-
tioning within a farm or a bay or a coastal area/region where multiple species are being
cultured. It is this inherent ﬂexibility and lack of clear spatial deﬁnition that can pose a
challenge to traditional aquaculture regulations and policy makers, but at the same time
allows for IMTA to be implemented under diﬀerent conditions and combinations in
coastal areas/regions, land-based operations and the open ocean.

Market security

Creating and developing pathways to new markets for value-added products from estab-
lished aquaculture industries (i.e. for additional species such as sea cucumbers or extracts
for nutraceuticals) are diﬃcult and this has constrained development in these sectors.
However, new directives are emerging in the industry through the Smart + Connected
aquaculture forum to seek pathways to develop coculture and to realise revenue from

Operational costs

The aquaculture industry has invested heavily in innovative technologies to reduce labour
costs for harvesting and post-harvest processing. Other costs relate to maintaining a
shellﬁsh quality assurance programme that meets the speciﬁcations and standards set
by the U.S Food and Drug Administration, European Union and New Zealand MPI.
Marine farmers are also subject to costs in utilising water space, through a variety of
consent charges, application fees, research costs, monitoring charges and bonds.

High value species and high value products

Commercial aquaculture in New Zealand is presently dominated by companies farming
single species. However, the industry is receptive to opportunities for multi-species
farming that are economically viable as exempliﬁed by many mussel farms being licenced
to culture other shellﬁsh (e.g. oysters and scallops) and seaweeds. While the inclusion of
seaweed (i.e. U. pinnatiﬁda) is gaining traction in the industry, species like sea cucumbers,
sponges and geoducks are receiving less attention, with only a few innovative companies
exploring opportunities to farm these high value species. Marine farmers are constantly
evaluating alternatives to diversify their operation be it individually or as a group.

The possibility to farm new ﬁnﬁsh species, namely yellowtail kingﬁsh and hāpuku,
could allow for multi-species production systems including seaweeds, mussels and sea
cucumbers to be developed as part of the environmental management requirements for
these farms. Nonetheless, mussel farming is still likely to remain the ﬂagship industry
in New Zealand. Conversion of some mussel farms to include U. pinnatiﬁda and other
extractive species such as sea cucumbers appears to be the best opportunity for IMTA
uptake at this time.

Land-based IMTA (marine and freshwater)

The development of land-based aquaculture, including recirculation systems in New
Zealand will allow the evaluation of a multitude of applications linking aquaculture
with other industries such as dairy and agriculture/horticulture (http://www.lbaaf.co.nz/
land-based-aquaculture/integrated-aquaculture/). A conventional combination would be
to use the waste generated by ﬁsh farming to enhance the production of primary producers
such as microalgae or seaweed in marine systems or vegetables in freshwater systems (i.e.
aquaponics, Nichols and Lennard 2010). Deposit-feeders such as sea cucumbers could be
included in land-based recirculation systems using seawater (e,g., abalone). Alternatively,
if the solid waste from the ﬁsh farm (i.e. hatchery) or abalone is collected, it could be used
as fertiliser for other crops on land. Dairy farms can be a source of nutrients for primary

Open ocean aquaculture
Recently, New Zealand has allocated ∼10,000 ha for the development of open ocean aqua-
culture at various sites around New Zealand, far from the sheltered coastline (see Figure 1)
(Goseberg et al. 2017). These new spaces have the potential for the inclusion or farming of
multiple species of shellﬁsh and seaweed, provided adequate system designs and manage-
ment protocols can be developed (Buck et al. 2017). Currently, a Government funded
project (Enabling Open Ocean Aquaculture – New Zealand MBIE) led by the Cawthron
Institute is working on the development and evaluation of systems that could integrate
diﬀerent shellﬁsh species (e.g. mussels, oysters, scallops) with seaweeds and sea cucumbers
at high energy marine farimng sites (Kevin Heasman pers. comm.)

The way forward

A clean environment and sustainable farming practices are seen by many as the corner-
stone of the New Zealand aquaculture industry. These are increasingly used to underpin
marketing strategies, based on high quality and wholesome product produced in a ‘clean
and green’ environment. Whilst a low population density and an extensive coastal area
have supported this image to date;
increasingly markets are demanding that such
claims can be demonstrated in ways that are meaningful to consumers. Aquaculture sus-
tainability research is seeking to quantify aquaculture and environment interactions in
ways that are meaningful for all aquaculture stakeholders and to assist the industry in
footprint. Ecological

areas. Achieving an optimal balance of biomass and spatial positioning will always be a
challenge but with each system developed, models can be reﬁned until ultimately, predic-
tions increase in reliability. However, industry ‘buy in’ to the use of IMTA technology will
be relaint on the practicality of the technology developed and by demonstrable ﬁnancial
beneﬁts, either through new products, improved market returns derived from its use or
the environmental mitigating role coculture species can play. These developments will
ultimately be industry driven.

Although aquaculture is a small sector of the New Zealand economy and is constrained
by space in which to operate, it has enormous potential to grow through innovation and
production of high-value products. Packaging of certiﬁed sustainable products, including
nutraceuticals and organic pharmaceuticals, with organic and/or IMTA labelling delivered
to an international market, is the pathway to realising an industry worth NZ$ 3 billion by
2035. The recent emergence of aquaculture fora where industry, researchers, and regula-
tors meet to discuss future research and development has ignited interest in diversiﬁcation
of the sector inclusive of IMTA opportunities. This momentum for change creates
