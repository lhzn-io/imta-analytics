Abstract
With  the  rapid  growth  of  aquaculture,  some  negative  factors  of  extensive  monoculture
accumulated,  causing  integrated  multi-trophic  aquaculture  (IMTA)  to  draw  increasing  attention
worldwide  for  its  ecological  and  economic  advantages.  However,  the  development  of  IMTA  in
open  water  systems  may  not  go  as  smoothly  as  anticipated—at  least  in  Weihai,  China,  some
producers  who  have  adopted  it  would  prefer  to  return  to  monoculture.  This  paper  explores  the
problem from the angle of the economic performance by providing an in-depth analysis of costs
and revenues generated by kelp monoculture and kelp-mollusk polyculture (IMTA). We find that
the  monoculture  generates  higher  profits  during  the  same  production  cycle.  Besides  inherent
defects  in  system  design,  the  rapidly  growing  labor  cost  and  depressing  selling  price  of  the
mollusk  conspire  to  aggravate  the  economic  failure  of  IMTA.  Due  to  positive  environmental
externalities,  the  social  benefits  associated  with  IMTA  are  higher  than  the private  benefits.  This
implies that there is a role for the government to generalize IMTA to achieve more total benefits.
But single policy such as subsidies may backfire, a combination of policies designed to promote
IMTA could be effective.

    The world’s fishery catches have declined from the peak in the 1990s to steady level, despite an

    IMTA is the polyculture of species with different  trophic niches, so one species’ uneaten feed
and  wastes,  nutrients  and  by-products  become  inputs  for  another,  which  has  been  gaining
recognition  as  a  sustainable  model  of  aquaculture  due  to  the  combination  of  environmental
sustainability,  economic  stability  and  societal  acceptability  (Nobre  et  al.,  2010;  Chopin  et  al.,
2006).  Research  projects  related  to  the  development  of  IMTA  have  taken  place  in  the  United
Kingdom,  Ireland,  Spain,  Portugal,  France,  Turkey,  Norway,  Japan,  Korea,  Thailand,  Canada,
United  States,  Mexico,  etc.  (Chopin  et  al.,  2012;  Handå  et  al.,  2012;  Ridler  et  al.,  2007;
Whitmarsh et al., 2006; Edwards, 2015). China also has a long history in this field, but the early
projects were mainly carried out for inland ponds. Authors such as Xie et al. (1981), Zhang et al.
(1985) and Chen et al. (1989) found that algae-shellfish polyculture resulted in higher yield. Yuan
et al. (1995) pointed out that turtle capturing dead fish and feeding on it in fish-turtle polyculture
system  would  reduce  farmer’s  operating  cost.  Li  et  al.  (2002)  found  that  shrimp-fish-scallop
polyculture  had  the  best  performance  in  terms  of  total  yield  and  the  utilization  of  nitrogen  by
testing  four  different  IMTA  modes  during  1995-1999.  After  2000,  related  research  started  to  be
developed in coastal waters of China. Luan et al. (2001) claimed that deep-water  IMTA had the
potential  for  replacing  the  existing  monoculture  practice  in  the  north  inshore  waters  of  China.
Wang  (2001)  advocated  developing  kelp-scallop  polyculture  because  it  contributes  to  reducing
farming  pollution  and  Zhang  et  al.  (2005)  further  proved  that  this  approach  could  enhance  the
capability of coastal water for absorbing carbon dioxide. Ou et al. (2011) reaffirmed kelp-abalone
polyculture  promoting  nutrient  substance  in  virtuous  circle.  However,  many  of  these  studies
achieved  their  theoretical  feasibility  through  small-scale  experiments,  while  lacked  practical
verification  of  whether  they  work  for  a  larger  scale.  In  recent  years,  the  ongoing  mariculture
expansion  and  resulting  tensions  of  inshore  space  in  China  have  provided  an  opportunity  for
empirical  research.  For  instance, Shi et  al.  (2013) conducted ecological-economic  assessment  of
monoculture  and  IMTA  according  to  the  actual  production  in  Sungo  Bay,  Weihai,  their  study
supported  the  successful  application  of  IMTA  in  open  water  systems  by  finding  significantly
higher sustainability in IMTA from both the environmental and economic aspects.
    But during our field surveys in late 2013 in Weihai, we surprisingly found that some producers
who  had  already  adopted  IMTA  planned  to  retreat  to  monoculture  for  the  future.  If  IMTA,  as
mentioned  above,  could  offer  an  opportunity  to  combine  social,  environmental  and  economic
advantages, why did they refuse this approach in such a win-win situation?
    Small-scale  independent  producers  have  little  incentive  to  consider  regional  or  coastal-wide
environmental impacts in their decision making process (Asche et al., 1999; Johnston et al., 2008).
―Adoption  will  occur,  only  if  economic  circumstances,  particularly,  price  trends,  are  favorable‖
(Whitmarsh  et  al.,  2006).  In  fact,  Shi  et  al.  (2013)  made  their  evaluation  through  sample  data
collected  from  the  survey  in  2009,  while  we  learned  of  the  producers’  plans  in  late  2013.
Obviously, there are time inconsistency and information gaps between different conclusions. It is
necessary  to  get  a  clear  picture  of  changed  operation  conditions  and  economic  performances,
particularly for the year 2013. Therefore, in March of 2014, we returned to Weihai to carry out a

    Apparently, we could perform comparison on economic performance between monoculture and
polyculture (IMTA) directly by following the approach of Shi et al. (2013) and updating sample
data. However, such a free riding is hardly feasible because the first thing we have to face is the
proverbial  ―chicken  or  egg‖  problem.  The  different  reference  object  here  draws  the  opposite
conclusion.  e.g.  Given  there  are  A  and B  monoculture  systems,  respectively,  for  two  species,  A
monoculture  has  significantly  higher  profitability  than  B  monoculture,  then  an  A-B  polyculture
will  probably  result  in  medium  economic  performance.  Comparing  to  A  monoculture,  A-B
polyculture  is  inferior  in  profitability;  while  comparing  to  B  monoculture,  it  can  be  regarded  as
very successful economically. Then which one makes economic sense? Should we compare in the
light of which species is farmed earlier or at a larger scale? So we need to step back and look at it
from systematic perspective. With information flows, producers do not permanently lock up their
investments into one species or one aquaculture mode. The nature of profit-seeking dictates that
they hinge upon profitability. This means that no matter how many species and which one is first
or most farmed, when we simply focus on the economic viability of IMTA, each polyculture here
should be compared with the most profitable monoculture. Owing to the foregoing fieldwork, we
have  confirmed  that  kelp  monoculture,  compared  with  scallop  monoculture  and  abalone
monoculture, was the most profitable in 2013 in Weihai. Therefore, in this paper, the comparison
on  monoculture  is  omitted  and  kelp  monoculture  is  set  as  the  reference  object,  compared  with
kelp-scallop  polyculture  and  kelp-abalone  polyculture  in  respect  of  the  profitability.  The  reason
that we choose these three species (instead of clam, oyster, etc.) is that they have been employed
both in monoculture and polyculture at great commercial scale in Weihai.
    To  conduct  such  comparison,  our  work  is  performed  in  the  following  four  steps:  (1)
questionnaire design; (2) data collection; (3) data processing; and (4) viability evaluation.

2.1. Questionnaire design

    Since few data are available on aquaculture at an individual level in China, the analysis of the
economic performance of producers has to be achieved through the acquisition of structural data.
It is necessary to conduct interviews through questionnaires (Bouwmans et al., 2015). To facilitate
this task, though there are different aquaculture modes involved, all our questions are integrated in
a semi-closed questionnaire.
    In  this questionnaire,  costs  and  revenues  are  two  major  questions. The  costs  are composed  of
four major components: facility cost, labor cost, seedling or juvenile cost, and other operation cost.
Except seedling or juvenile cost, each component consists of various subcomponents. Facility cost

and truck) cost. Each item is described in terms of quantity, unit cost and service life in row. Since

2.2. Data collection

    Through  the  recommendation  of  the  local  fisheries  sector,  our  survey  started  out  with  two
enterprises.  Subsequently,  samples  increased  to  five  owing  to  referrals  from  the  existing
interviewees. However, we soon realized the inherent disadvantage of such a snowball sampling.
The  respondents  were  mostly  ―elites‖,  for  instance,  the  first  two,  respectively,  held  4,500  mu  (a
customary  Chinese unit  of  area, 1  mu  approximates  0.067  hectare)  and  20,000 mu  of  registered
sea area, 2,700 mu and 12,000 mu of farming sea area. This kind of scale, even the smaller one,
was uncommon for aquaculture in China.
    To  avoid  missing  the information  of  small-scale  farmers (whose  farming  sea area  is, say,  less
than 50 mu), additional five samples showing household-level farms were randomly selected after
we received the list from village or town committees.
    Total  ten  samples  have  been  collected,  with  100%  response  due  to  the  adoption  of  in-person
interviews.  Three  of  them  are  in  Huancui  district  and  the  other  seven  are  in  Rongcheng  county
(Fig.1).  There  are  five  samples  relevant  for  kelp  monoculture,  four  relevant  for  kelp-scallop
polyculture, seven for kelp-abalone polyculture and two for all the modes.

2.3. Data processing

    As  mentioned  above  some  producers  adopt  diversified  operations,  but  they  seldom  make
separated accounting statements, which means that some inputs of different aquaculture modes are

Though  IMTA  may  not  achieve  the  maximum  economic  benefits,  it  generates  greater  total
benefits for society by its ecosystem goods and services. This typical externality problem around
IMTA is described in Fig. 3. Producers face downward-sloping private marginal benefit curves as
selling  price  decrease  with  increasing  production.  The  (private)  cost  from  operation  input  is
initially low and then rises. This leads to an equilibrium at the point X where cost equals benefit
and determines the optimal IMTA quantity for operators at Q1. Since producers cannot charge for
positive  externality  of  IMTA,  their  benefit  curves  would  be  lower  than  social  benefit  curves.
Similarly,  the  optimal  IMTA  quantity  for  society  is  at  Q2,  reflecting  there  is  insufficient  market
demand (Q1 < Q2) on IMTA.
    For  externality,  the  normal  route  is  tax  inputs  that  increase  a  detrimental  externality  and
subsidize  inputs  that  reduce  it  (Griffin  and  Bromley,  1982).  Then  let’s  assume  that  government
provided  subsidies  for  IMTA,  producers’  cost  at  the  same  yield  would  go  down  and  the
equilibrium in Fig. 4 would shift lower right from the point X to the point Y. With subsidies, the
quantity  of  IMTA  would  increase  from  Q1  to  Q2  and  greater  production  could  be  realized.  But
production  could  also  be  the  chief  culprit  of  price  depression/drop  (e.g.  Chinese  scallop  and
abalone),  subsidies  may  fuel  up  price  falling  in  the  long  run.  Nevertheless,  there  is  a  role  for
government since the social benefits associated with IMTA are higher than the private benefits.
    Probably no single government policy, or managerial practice, will be adequate to generalized
IMTA  in  such  a  dilemma.  As  traditional  Chinese  medicine  theories  suggest,  systemic  problems
should  be  treated  through  systemic  methods.  Besides  incentives  building  on  the  momentum  of
producer-led  initiatives,  three  logical  adjuvants  stand  out  in  setting  of  IMTA.  First,  better
valuation  of  the  private  and  social  non-market  benefits  is  needed.  In  ecological  and  economic
win-win  situation  IMTA  is  no  doubt  accepted,  but  when  it  is  at  economic  disadvantages  (e.g.
kelp-scallop/abalone  polyculture),  IMTA  may  not  been  implemented  successfully.  Once  the
correlation  between  non-market  benefits  and  market  values  is  established,  the  total  returns  of
IMTA  are  quantifiable.  Then  government  can  have  a  better  idea  of  how  much  it  would  offer  to
generalize this pattern.
    Second,  perfecting  information  system  should  be  actualized  quickly.  The  reality  of  imperfect
and  incomplete  information  about  IMTA  magnifies  the  problem  of  management  absence  or
mismanagement. Some reports eulogize production or scale while neglecting profitability also add
a  misunderstanding  of  current  situation.  Besides,  information  asymmetry  may  lead  to  adverse
selection6  and make producers hard to get the price premium. Consumers will have a hard time
visually distinguishing which products are from IMTA so certification is necessary. Clearly it is a
long-run approach which also aims to improve consumer awareness and producer attitude.

    Third,  a  safeguard  mechanism  could  be  incorporated  in  the  implementation.  The  lack  of

5. Conclusions

    Aquaculture continues to expand quickly worldwide to meet the increasing demand for aquatic
products. Meanwhile, many negative factors around disease, environment, biodiversity, and so on
appear  in  large-scale  monoculture.  As  the  most  commonly  discussed  solution,  IMTA  was
considered  to  have  environmental  sustainability,  economic  stability  and  societal  acceptability
(Chopin et al., 2008; Martínez-Espiñeira et al., 2015). But the realistic development of IMTA in
the  open  water  farming  approaches  might  not  go  as  smoothly  as  anticipated  since some farmers
who adopted it in Weihai, China would return to monoculture mode.
    Through  the  breakdown  of  costs  and  revenues,  the  profit  analyses  around  some  real  cases
carried out  in  this paper  verified that  economic  performance was  the main  reason  for  the  recent
decline  of  IMTA  development.  Besides  some  flaw  in  design,  the  fast  growing  labor  cost  and
depressed  selling  price  conspired  to  poor  economic  efficiency  of  kelp-scallop  and  kelp-abalone
IMTA production modes.
    If simply compared with monoculture for the most profitable species, IMTA is often at inferior
or neutral position in economic results since its great ecological benefits are unquantifiable. That
is also why total private benefits are lower than social benefits. IMTA should not be approached
with the ―out of sight, out of mind‖ attitude, but pushed by the government because such positive
externalities payable shall be accounted at public sectors.
    But  single  policy  such  as  subsides  may  backfire.  Therefore,  to  solve  this  problem,  a
combination of several research and policy instruments that include the valuation on non-market
benefits of IMTA, improvement of information system and construction for safeguard mechanism
should be implemented.
