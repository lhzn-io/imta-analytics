ABSTRACT
Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) is the farming, in
proximity, of aquaculture species from different trophic levels
and with complementary ecosystem functions. IMTA allows one
species’ uneaten feed and wastes, nutrients, and by-products to
be recaptured and converted into fertilizer, feed, and energy for
the other crops. By taking advantage of synergistic interactions
between species,
IMTA can help aquaculture evolve towards
more responsible and sustainable systems. This study uses data
from a contingent valuation survey to provide an estimation of
the non-use benefits that, in the form of biomitigation of the
external costs imposed on the marine environment, would be
derived by Canadians from the adoption of IMTA for Atlantic
salmon aquaculture. We find the benefits accruing to households
who do not purchase salmon habitually would range between
about $43 million/year and about $65 million/year for the next
five years, depending on the treatment of ‘don’t know’ responses
to the payment question.

Introduction

Facing an increasing demand for seafood and a declining availability of wild
species and stocks, the world is increasingly turning to aquaculture as the main
source of seafood supply. Globally, the production through aquaculture of all
aquatic species has grown at an average rate of nearly 10% a year for the last
three decades (Ayer et al., 2009), accounting now for about half of the total
seafood used for direct human consumption (FAO, 2011).

However, concerns have also been expressed about the environmental
impacts of aquaculture (e.g., Olesen et al., 2011). These include discharge of
(mainly nitrogen and
wastes,
phosphorus), pathogens and chemicals, and the potential impact that the

To continue to grow, the aquaculture sector needs to develop more innova-
tive, responsible, sustainable and profitable technologies and practices.
Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) is a practice that has the poten-
tial to play a role in reaching this objective (Chopin, 2013). IMTA consists of
farming in proximity aquaculture species from different trophic levels and with
complementary ecosystem functions. This strategy makes it possible for one
species’ uneaten feed and wastes, nutrients, and by-products to be recaptured
and turned into fertilizer, feed, and energy for the other crops, exploiting
synergistic interactions between the different species. IMTA farmers combine
fed aquaculture (e.g., finfish or shrimps) with extractive aquaculture, which
utilizes the inorganic (e.g., seaweeds or other aquatic vegetation) and organic
(e.g., suspension-and deposit-feeders) excess nutrients from fed aquaculture
for their growth. The aim of IMTA is to foster environmental sustainability
(through biomitigative services for improved ecosystem health), economic
profitability and stability (through improved outputs, lower costs, product
diversification, risk reductions, and job creation in rural and coastal com-
munities) and social acceptability (by adopting better management practices,
improved regulatory governance and appreciation of differentiated and safe
products).

This study estimates the non-use benefits, in the form of biomitigation of
the external costs imposed on the marine environment, that would be derived
by Canadians from the adoption of IMTA techniques in the farming of
Atlantic salmon (one of the most commonly purchased products of aquacul-
ture in North America). These non-use benefits of IMTA were estimated using
a Contingent Valuation (CV) survey targeted at members of the general public
who had not consumed farmed salmon within the previous 12 months.1 The
survey asked about households’ willingness to support, through a portion of
their annual taxes, a hypothetical policy that would subsidize the adoption
of IMTA production techniques by 50% of the current number of Atlantic
salmon farmers, which would lead to a certain proportion of waste reduction
with respect to conventional aquaculture.

Although any potential increase in production costs imposed by the adop-
tion of IMTA techniques could be presumably recouped through increased
output prices reflecting increased use values due to the availability of a
credence attribute, as well as by the added productivity in terms of shell-
fish/algae or an enhanced demand for these other products when produced
through IMTA, any non-use values generated by IMTA would be ignored
in an unregulated market. This exercise makes it possible to estimate aggre-
gate measures of increases in non-use values that could be compared with
the cost of effecting market interventions aimed at promoting the adoption
of IMTA production techniques, in order to approximate the social optimum

Background

Muir et al. (1999) discuss a variety of techniques used in environmental
economics that can be applied to the estimation of the non-market economic
impacts of aquaculture, with a special focus on stated preference methods and
hedonic property value methods. While hedonic methods are most useful
when assessing the value of localized environmental externalities that affect
quality of life in coastal regions, as reflected in local property values, stated
preference methods are best suited, as pointed out by Johnston et al. (2008),
to assessing the total economic values (positive or negative) derived from
aquaculture production. Although numerous studies have addressed the
general demand for aquaculture products, dealing with different aspects of
aquaculture production and benefits, studies that focus on the willingness to
pay for positive environmental effects of the choices made by aquaculture
producers are more scarce.

Whitmarsh and Wattage (2006) identify the challenges of measuring the
economic value of the externalities imposed by aquaculture. They adminis-
tered a mail questionnaire in Scotland based on the Analytic Hierarchy Process
and CV (open-ended question format) approaches. Respondents were told that
one way in which salmon farming affected the environment was by discharging
nutrients into the sea, causing a decline in water quality. Then it was suggested
to respondents that it may be possible to farm salmon so as to reduce these
discharges but that to do so might raise the price of salmon. They were then
asked whether they would be willing to pay more for salmon farmed using a
method that caused 50% less organic pollution, and if so how much more.
The survey revealed that some 76% of respondents were in principle willing
to pay a premium for salmon produced using such a method. Overall, the aver-
age amount that people were prepared to pay above the price of conventional
salmon was 22% (measured as the ‘trimmed’ mean value).

Some research results from Canada are available based on attitudinal studies
towards salmon farming in general, and IMTA in particular (Ridler et al., 2006;
Ridler et al., 2007a). These studies, all of which centered on the development of
the IMTA technology on the East Coast of Canada, found that the general
public is more negative towards current monoculture practices and feels rather
positive about IMTA, and that this production technique results in increased
profitability and reduced risks for the private operator (Ridler et al., 2007a,
2007b).

Barrington et al. (2010) focused on IMTA salmon production in New
Brunswick, surveying groups of restaurateurs, residents of communities
located close to aquaculture facilities, and members of the general public. Most

All of these studies based on the Canadian context provide estimates of the
benefits that IMTA would afford to a private operator either directly in terms
of reduced production costs or indirectly, through the effect on demand for
the product of a favorable consumer perception of the IMTA techniques.
There is, however, no precedent of a Canadian study that estimates the ben-
efits to society of the external effects of introducing IMTA. In general, there
are only very few estimates of the value of the biomitigation services of IMTA
in any jurisdiction. Some of the exceptions (discussed in more detail in the
Results Section) would be Chopin et al. (2001), Nobre et al. (2010) and Shi
et al. (2013). Furthermore, these studies involve estimations of the value of
the ecosystem services associated with the external effects of IMTA not
directly but transferring estimates obtained in previous studies of the costs
of nutrient removal, restoration costs, and emission offset rates. Additionally,
all of these previous studies valued different types of externalities under very
different conditions and in different geographical areas, making comparison
exercises with the Canadian case or benefit-transfer attempts difficult; hence,
the need remains for a new study based primarily on Canadian data.

The contingent valuation method

The Contingent Valuation Method (CVM) is a standard approach to elicit
WTP. It is most often based on close-ended, dichotomous choice, questions
included in a hypothetical market-type survey, which can be administered
via telephone, mail, or in person (Kanninen, 1993). When the single-bounded
dichotomous choice CVM is used, each respondent is asked whether or not
he/she would be willing to pay a particular bid for a particular good in a hypo-
thetical market. The respondent must choose among a ‘yes’, a ‘no’, and usually
a ‘don’t know’ and the proportions of these responses can be tracked in relation
to the size of the “bid” amounts offered to each individual.

As a stated preference (as opposed to a revealed preference) method, the
CVM can be used to estimate the value of non-market public goods that
generate non-use values (Mitchell & Carson, 1989; Bateman et al., 2002).
For example, it can be used to estimate the economic benefits derived from
a clean environment. However, although the main advantage of the CVM is
its ability to deal with non-marketed goods, the method can also be exploited
to estimate the value of private goods. The CVM is especially useful in those
cases when some aspect of the good or service concerned does not currently
have a market expression (Whitmarsh & Wattage, 2006). For example, the
CVM has been used in this way to investigate the demand for eco-labeled sea-
food, showing that consumers are willing to pay a price premium for seafood
safety assurances (Wessells & Anderson 1995), for fish certified as originating
from a ‘sustainably managed’ fishery (Young et al., 1999; Pickering, 2001;
Whitmarsh & Wattage, 2006), and for fish welfare itself (Solgaard & Yang,
2011).

Data

Data were collected through a hybrid phone/Internet survey2 administered to a
sample of adult Canadians. The sampling effort resulted in a total sample size
of 1,197 usable responses (out of a total of 1,246 responses). The response rate
was 18% after up to three reminders.3 As in Whitehead et al. (2003), we used
two different versions of the questionnaire, one based on the CVM and the
other on the Contingent Behavior Method (CBM), to, in our case, estimate
the benefits of the adoption of IMTA production. Respondents whose house-
hold had not purchased any farmed Atlantic salmon during the previous
twelve months were allocated the CV version of the questionnaire. This con-
tribution deals only with this subsample. The questionnaire was pre-tested
on about 100 subjects, in order to determine its plausibility and understand-
ability and in order to adjust the range of the bid vector in the CV version.

The CV version of

the survey instrument collected information on
demographic characteristics, and attitudes towards eco-labels, apart from the
questions about WTP for a policy that promoted the production of IMTA
salmon.

Before the questions about WTP, respondents were also asked about their
awareness of IMTA. Their categorical responses were coded as the ordinal
variable “heard2”.4 Since IMTA is a relatively new technology, most respon-
dents were not familiar with it.

To motivate the payment questions, a policy scenario5 was presented which
briefly described the advantages of IMTA in generic terms, suggesting that the
adoption of IMTA techniques by salmon farms would lead to a certain
proportional reduction6 in their waste generation. The payment scenario
was kept simple in order to facilitate the task for the respondents. We, in

The variety of strategies and results found in the literature10 suggests that
there is no consensus about how to treat “don’t know/not sure/would not

directly with binary choice models.

Econometric analysis

The main goal of a CV exercise is to estimate summary welfare measures
(mean and median WTP, most usually) that describe the benefit derived from
the valued good or service, in our case the biomitigative services of IMTA
salmon production. However, even when the main objective is the estimation
of some summary value of the distribution of the WTP variable, which could
be achieved through non-parametric methods, regression analysis is usually
employed. Estimated relationships can help establish further confidence on
the valuation exercise, as they permit us to check that the level of WTP varies

Eliminating ‘don’t know’ responses

Another very common alternative for treatment of the DK responses is to
simply drop them from the sample. In principle, this should lead to an
increase in the precision of the estimation of mean WTP. This is because, after
all, people who respond “DK” must have had a harder time completing the
questionnaire than those others who responded with a clear ‘yes’ or ‘no’,
which suggests that the former have more trouble identifying their maximum
WTP in support of the policy. We also should expect an increase in the mean
WTP estimate, since this time DK answers are not recoded as zeros, having
instead their influence eliminated from the analysis. Therefore, we should
expect a reduction in the ratio of the width of the 95% confidence interval
to the mean WTP.

Variable WTPq was built from the WTP question by dropping the orig-
inal 124 DK and ‘no response’ cases. The results of regressing this variable
on the same regressors as above are reported in the third and fourth col-
umns of Table 8. Just as in the previous case, testing the joint significance
of male, age, age × age, scope, college, hunter, fisher, and member leads to
non-rejection of the null hypothesis, so we report both a full model and a
simpler model. The resulting mean WTP estimates are about twice as large
as in the models that considered DK responses equivalent to ‘no’s’. These
regressions also yield a much narrower 95% confidence interval, above
all when compared to the, now larger, size of the mean WTP. The combi-
nation of a larger estimate of WTP and its increased precision lead us to
clearly reject that the mean WTP could be zero (almost at a 0.1% level of
significance). Eliminating DK responses appears to have helped with the
estimation of the bid parameter and, by extension, with the estimation of
the mean WTP, but the results in terms of the other covariates remained
largely unaffected.

Comparison with earlier benefit estimates

Several studies have valued the externalities associated with aquaculture
practices other than IMTA, such as Zheng et al. (2009), who valued the
largely positive impacts on ecosystem services of mariculture in Sanggou
Bay, China, or Ferreira et al. (2009), who estimated that coastal EU shellfish
aquaculture provided an estimated net value of $15.3 to $23.6 billion per year
in ecosystem goods and services (eutrophication reduction). However, analy-
ses of IMTA systems that account for external costs and benefits are very
scarce, in particular those that are not focused on estimating the premia that
seafood consumers would pay for the IMTA version of a given product.

15

As an exception, Chopin et al. (2001), using solid and dissolved nitrogen and
phosphorus production volumes from the site and waste treatment costs from
Folke et al. (1994),19 valued the environmental savings due to the reduction in
nutrient loading when an IMTA configuration was adopted in a salmon farm
in Chile. They found these savings to amount to about 70% or about $137,000
for 250 tons of gross fish production. Roughly extrapolated to the current level
of farmed salmon production in Canada (about 125,000 tons/year), this would
add up to savings of around $70 million per year. This crude approximation
(not considering additional types of ecosystem services, abstracting from
inflation effects, etc.) appears to result in a comparable figure to the one we
obtained in our study for the external benefits of the use of IMTA in salmon
farming.

However, we note that Chopin et al. (2001), as also, more recently, Nobre et
al. (2010) and Shi et al. (2013) did not estimate the value of the external effects
of IMTA directly, but based, instead, on nutrient removal cost estimates,
restoration costs, and emission offset rates estimated by others. This, together
with the notion that all these previous studies, in fact, valued different types of
externalities under different contexts, complicates any meaningful direct com-
parison with our own results and suggests that a benefit-transfer approach
would not be entirely possible at this stage.

Finally, a comparison could be made between our results and the benefits of
IMTA production estimated through the contingent behavior analysis conduc-
ted on the subsample of respondents who stated that they had purchased
farmed Atlantic salmon in the previous twelve months (Martinez-Espiñeira
et al. 2015). The higher values yielded by the contingent behavior study20
highlight the fact that considering the environmental effects of one’s con-
sumption (even if hypothetical) as a regular consumer of a product purchased
in a supermarket can be quite different from considering one’s benefit from
a public policy that addresses those environmental impacts, being a consumer
or not.

Analysis of effects of non-price covariates

The estimates in Table 8 show the effects of variables other that the proposed
bid on the willingness to support the promotion of IMTA techniques.
Although studies such as Wessells et al. (1999) found that women are more
likely to choose environmentally labeled products (eco-labeled seafood in their
case), there seem to be no observable gender effects on WTP according to our
estimations. However, household size appears to exert a positive and signifi-
cant effect. Only in the models (not reported but available upon request) that
deal with a sample with protest responses excluded, could we also detect a
significantly positive effect of higher education (variable college), while the
effect is no longer significant when the protest responses are included, which

suggests that university educated respondents were relatively likely to respond
with a protest ‘no’ to the WTP question. Not surprisingly, those who consider
themselves to be among the first to try new food products are also more likely
to support the suggested policy through their taxes. Also the more consumers
had seen, heard, or read about IMTA before completing the questionnaire the
higher their WTP for the policy.

We found that the most likely consumers to accept the bid offered in sup-
port for the policy were those falling in the second income bracket (between
$10,000 and $19,999), which suggests in principle that WTP for the biomitiga-
tion services of IMTA is not positively correlated with income. However, the
households included in that bracket corresponded predominantly to respon-
dents over the age of 65, that is, likely retired, and for whom ‘household
income’ would not appropriately capture purchasing power.

There is a clear negative effect of having children in the household on
WTP, perhaps suggesting that the current understanding of IMTA might
be mistakenly associated by some with genetic modification of the fish or with
some other process potentially damaging to one’s (or one’s children) health.21
This would mean that parents are less prone to contribute to the public good
that biomitigation represents if they think that in the process they could be
jeopardizing their own children’s health. This is in line with the results
obtained by Magnusson and Cranfield (2005) who also found that Canadian
families with children would generally be willing to pay more for pesticide-
free food products.

The effect of the numerical certainty scale “sure2” is ambiguous, depending
on whether DK responses are considered zeros or missing values. In the former
case, since DK responses will appear as very unsure zeros, the effect of the scale
is positive and significant. However, when DK responses are dropped as miss-
ing values, the scale is negatively related (if much less strongly) to the WTP.

Conclusions, limitations and suggestions for further research

The objective of this study was the estimation of the non-use benefits, in the
form of decreased external costs imposed on the marine environment, that
would be derived by Canadians from the adoption of IMTA techniques in
Atlantic salmon farming or, by extension, the adoption of any production
technology (other than IMTA specifically) that secures a similar environmen-
tal performance. Our estimations yield a lower bound for this benefit to
households who do not purchase salmon habitually of about $43 [$8,$69]
million/year for the next five years.

This figure is the result of treating DK responses as valid ‘no’s’, while
assuming that protest responses could be also treated as regular ‘no’s’. These
treatment decisions were informed by the postestimation analysis of a multi-
nomial logit regression with only the level of the bid as a covariate. However,

it is likely that, although protest and DK responses cannot be distinguished
from each other and from regular ‘no’ responses when modelled as a function
of only the bid, they become more distinguishable once additional covariates
are introduced. Therefore, it would be desirable to explore additional models
that deal with protests and DK responses in alternative ways. In this paper, we
show that dropping them as missing values instead would yield a benefit to
households who do not purchase salmon habitually of around $65[$34,$85]
million/year for the next five years. However, a further avenue of research
would involve misclassification analysis of the DK responses, as well as the
analysis of the effects of different recoding strategies of this ambivalent
responses on the size and precision of the welfare estimates.

Additionally, although this paper followed the conservative approach of not
removing protest responses from the sample, it would be interesting to com-
pare how different treatments of protests could affect estimates of WTP,
beyond the obviously expected increase due to their removal. A companion
paper will be focusing on an analysis of the protests, while this contribution
has focused on the effect of removing DK responses.

Finally, although we used the value of the numerical certainty scale as an
independent variable in the WTP regressions, the information it contains
could be further exploited in a fuller treatment of uncertainty. For example,
the certainty scale could be used to weight the observations, which should lead
to an intermediate estimate of WTP between the two general cases reported
here (DK responses as zeros versus eliminated from the sample).

Payment scenario

The following text was used in the questionnaire in order to introduce the
policy scenario:

According to some recent research, one way to address some of the negative
impacts of aquaculture on the environment is to use IMTA. IMTA combines the
aquaculture of species that are fed, like salmon, with species that extract their food
from the seawater, like shellfish and seaweeds. In that way, the extra food and the
wastes of the first type of organisms are not lost but recaptured and converted into
food for other crops. They can then be harvested and marketed as healthy seafood,
while mitigation takes place (removal of nutrients and CO2 and supply of oxygen).
Consequently, all the different species have a commercial value as well as a key role
in recycling processes and services, making the overall aquaculture system more
sustainable.
