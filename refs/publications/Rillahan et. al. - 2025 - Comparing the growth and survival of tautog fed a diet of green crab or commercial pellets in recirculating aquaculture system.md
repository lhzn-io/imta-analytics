Tautog, a coastal marine wrasse, is a highly sought food fish in southern New England and the Mid-Atlantic
region of the United States due to its firm white fillet. Tautog has been identified as a candidate species for
marine aquaculture in the region. Unfortunately, previous grow out studies have documented slow growth,
which is detrimental for aquaculture. However, these studies contrast with data from wild populations which
indicate rapid growth in the first few years of life. It is theorized that these differences in growth could be due to
available diets. In the wild, tautog primarily prey on mollusks and crustacean prey, while laboratory studies rely
on commercial fishmeal-based diets. This study compared the growth and survival of juvenile tautog fed with
chopped green crabs, commercial fish pellets and a hybrid diet consisting of both green crabs and pellets. Wild
juvenile tautog were collected from local waters and cultivated in a recirculating aquaculture system for 250
days. Tautog fed the commercial pellet diet had a growth rate of 0.31 ± 0.11 mm/day, consistent with previous
grow out studies. The average growth rate of tautog fed the green crab diet was 0.52 ± 0.13 mm/day, with a
peak growth rate of 0.66 ± 0.07 mm/day. The hybrid diet was intermediate to the other treatments at an average
growth rate of 0.46 ± 0.1 mm/day. Statistically significant differences were observed across treatment groups (P
< 0.05). Survival was high in the green crab and hybrid treatments (96.7% ± 1.2% and 96.0% ± 2.0%,
respectively), and lower in the pellet treatment (85.3% ± 9.5 %). Data from this study indicate that the growth
potential of tautog is higher than previously documented in aquaculture studies. This study highlights the need
for additional research to develop feed formulations for non-piscivorous marine carnivores.

1. Introduction

The development of new aquaculture species is paramount to the
expansion of the domestic aquaculture industry. Currently there are only
a handful of well-developed aquaculture species (i.e., Atlantic salmon,
trout, shrimp, tilapia, etc.), few of which are well suited to the Southern
New England (US) environment. Developing local marine species will
increase regional aquaculture production which will relieve pressure on
wild populations, increase employment and economic opportunities,
enhance food security, as well as increase the overall production of
seafood for local and export markets (Halwart et al., 2003).

Tautog (Tautoga onitis), also known as blackfish, is a coastal wrasse
species whose range extends from Nova Scotia in Canada to South

Carolina in the US, with the highest concentrations from Cape Cod to the
Delaware Capes (Collette & Klein-MacPhee, 2002). Throughout their
range, tautog is a highly sought-after species by commercial and recre-
ational anglers. Tautog have a firm white fillet which commands a high
market price (average US$8.51/kg live weight ex-vessel price along the
Atlantic Coast between 2017 and 2021 (Atlantic Coastal Cooperative
Statistics Program, 2021). While this is a lucrative fishery, supply is
limited due to the current state of the wild fish resource. The Atlantic
States Marine Fisheries Commission (ASMFC) stock assessment has
historically identified tautog as overfished, with overfishing occurring
throughout much of the range (ASMFC, 2016). Recent updates have
shown improved stocks, however abundance is still below the target
spawning stock biomass (SSB), resulting in limitations on the wild

Tautog are robust and hardy fish, having a wide temperature (4(cid:0) 30
C) and salinity tolerance (Steimle & Shaheen, 1999). Tautog can
tolerate high-density culture due to their life history as a reef-oriented
species with strong site fidelity and low movement rates (Collette &
Klein-MacPhee, 2002; Olla et al., 1974; Steimle & Shaheen, 1999). Life
history characteristics gave motivation for the development of this
species for aquaculture. The early excitement of tautog aquaculture
came from previous ecological work which indicated that juveniles
appeared to exhibit high growth rates (Cooper, 1967; Hostetter et al.,
1993; Martin, 1993).

Spawning induction and larval culture had been successful in the
laboratory (Olla & Samet, 1978; Perry et al., 1998, 2001, 2008). While
the early life history work has been well studied, these trials docu-
mented relatively slow growth. Perry et al. (2001) was able to spawn
and grow juvenile tautog for 490 days. During that study, growth
appeared to be linear from day 172–490 at a rate of 0.29 mm/day.
Similar growth rates were observed at the University of Rhode Island
(0.35 mm/day, Perry et al., 2001). This contrasts with numerous studies
of observed growth in the wild. Sogard (1992) observed juvenile tautog
growing at an average of 0.5 mm/day in New Jersey. Dorf (1994)
likewise observed growth rates of 0.54 mm/day in Narragansett Bay,
Rhode Island. While Martin (1993) observed similar growth in northern
fish (0.5 mm/day in New Jersey and Rhode Island), fish in Virginia
displayed growth rates of 1 mm/day, double that of their northern
counterparts and 3–4 times that of those grown in the laboratory. The
difference in growth rates between northern and southern fish may be
due to the prolonged growing season in warmer waters in the south
(Mercaldo-Allen et al., 2006; Steimle & Shaheen, 1999). The difference
in growth rates between laboratory and wild fish is highly unusual.
Perry et al. (2001) speculated that this could be due to an unknown
missing nutritional component in the feed in the laboratory. Fish in the
laboratory were fed a standard high protein commercial diet. These diets
are typical for most commercial aquaculture species. However, most
commercial aquaculture species are piscivorous, unlike tautog which are
generalist feeders whose diet primarily consists of mollusks and crus-
taceans in the wild (Clark et al., 2006; Olla et al., 1974). Young of the
year tautog have been raised in the laboratory at a growth rate of 0.7
mm/day on a diet of mysids (Martin, 1993). This indicates that alter-
native feed ingredients may yield significantly improved growth rates.
The present study examined the growth of tautog fed a raw crusta-
cean diet compared to a traditional commercial marine pellet diet. The
crustacean diet we chose for our study was green crabs, Carcinus maenas,
an invasive species commonly found along the northwest Atlantic and
eastern Pacific coasts. The establishment of this invasive species has
resulted in a significant regional ecological disruption causing damage
to local mussel beds, oyster reefs and eelgrass beds (Grosholz & Ruiz,
1995; Young et al., 2017). Most notable has been its local impact on the
commercially important soft-shell clam, Mya arenaria. The green crab is
a voracious predator of soft-shell clams consuming up to 21.8 clam-
s/crab/day (Floyd & Williams, 2004). In Maine and Massachusetts, the
annual harvest of soft-shell clam has been shown to be inversely related
to the abundance of green crabs (Young et al., 2017). Several programs
have been established in New England to actively reduce green crab
populations. Additionally, there has been an effort to create a market for
green crab products albeit with limited success. Most of green crabs are
therefore thrown into landfills or composted. Green crabs have been
reported to be a suitable candidate for fish-meal replacement (Fulton &
Fairchild, 2013). Integrating green crab into aquaculture feeds could
therefore provide economic, societal, and ecological benefits. This study
is the first evaluation of the growth characteristics and survival of tautog
fed a green crab diet.

2.2. Feeding trials

Following the acclimation period, fish were randomly divided into
nine separate 1900 L tanks and assigned one of three different feeding
treatments: green crab, control (commercial feed marine finfish diet), or
a hybrid diet (green crabs supplemented with commercial feed). The
hybrid diet was used to assess potential micro-nutrient limitation spec-
ulated by Perry et al. (2001). Fifty individuals were randomly placed in
each tank at an initial stock density of ~0.2 kg/m3. Each treatment was
maintained in triplicate, resulting in nine tanks total. Tanks were placed
on a recirculating aquaculture system with 10% daily water replace-
ment. The replicate tanks had controlled temperature (~20 C), salinity
(30–32 psμ), lighting (12 L:12D), and aeration. Flow rate was 15 L per
minute. In the wild, tautog are commonly associated with structured
habitats. As a result, three clusters of PVC pipes (6 pipes, 10 cm diameter
and 25 cm long) were placed in each tank. All tautog from each replicate
were sampled for total length (cm) and weight (g) approximately every
thirty days. In total there were 9 sampling periods spanning 250 days.
Feeding amounts were determined based on satiation and adminis-
tered at each feeding interval. Increases in feeding were decided based
on percent growth,

in the tanks after feeding. For the commercial pellets, satiation was
observed to be ~2% of the total body weight per day. Pellet sizes were
adjusted to account for the growth of the fish based on recommendations
from the manufacturer. Due to the high water content and shell mass,

3. Results

Water quality parameters were maintained at consistent levels across
tanks throughout the duration of the study. This included pH (7.5 ±
0.1), salinity (30.0 ± 2.0 psμ) and temperature (19.6 ± 1.4
C). The
initial distribution of fish lengths (7.86 ± 1.1 cm) and weights (7.30 ±
3.54 g) were similar across treatments and tanks at the initiation of the
experiment (P = 0.9219 and 0.9835, length and weight respectively,
Fig. 1). Survival of tautog throughout the duration of the experiment
was highest in the crab (96.7% ± 1.2%) and hybrid treatments (96.0%
± 2.0%) with reduced survival observed in the pellet treatment (85.3%
± 9.5%). All mortality observed in the study occurred within the first
100 days.

At the termination of the experiment, tautog length was significantly
greater in the crab treatment (21.0 ± 2.5 cm), compared to the hybrid
treatment (19.3 ± 2.2 cm, P = 0.0191) or pellet treatment (15.5 ± 2.5
cm, P < 0.0001, Fig. 1). LME models indicated that significant differ-

sampling period (P < 0.05). Differences between the crab and hybrid
diets were statistically significant after the second sampling period. The
average growth rate observed over the duration of the study was 0.52 ±
0.13 mm/day in the crab treatment, 0.46 ± 0.1 mm/day in the hybrid

growth rates assume linear growth, however intermediate sampling data
as well as the VBGM models indicate a non-linear growth. Growth rates
were observed to increase at the beginning of the experiment, peak
during the middle of the experiment and were reduced toward the end of
the experiment. For example, growth rates in the crab treatment
increased from 0.54 ± 0.03 mm/day during the first sampling period

were observed in the other two treatments. Growth rates in the hybrid
treatment peaked at 0.58 ± 0.15 mm/day during the fourth sampling

4. Discussion

This study was the first to evaluate the growth and survival of
cultured tautog fed a green crab diet. Data from this study indicate that
the green crab diet improved growth characteristics of cultured tautog.
At the termination of the study, fish fed the green crab diet were, on
average, twice as heavy as those fed the pellet diet. Average growth rates
in this study were 0.52 mm/day with a peak growth rate of 0.66 mm/
day in the crab treatment. The improved growth rates observed in this

(2001, p. 0.29 and 0.35 mm/day).

Perry et al. (2001) speculated that difference in the observed growth
rate between lab-reared and wild fish could be due to a nutritional
deficiency in the commercial feed. Proximate analysis of the crab and
pellet diets indicate a similar crude protein and fat composition once
moisture and ash (76% of the crab diet) are normalized. Additionally,
Fulton and Fairchild (2013) documented large-scale similarities in the
amino acid and lipid profiles of green crab compared to menhaden,
despite some specific differences, for example green crabs are lower in
lysine and tryptophan and higher in valine. Furthermore, Fulton and
Fairchild (2013) documented the largest amino acid components of

which have been observed to be important finfish feeding stimulants
(Carr et al., 1996). Previous feeding stimulation studies have docu-
mented the variable role of amino acid profiles between species and in
relation to known prey items (Kasumyan & Doving, 2003; Ols´en &
Lundh, 2016). As such, the differences in the observed growth rates may
be due to preferential palatability between the diets.

The palatability of a food is based on both attraction and ingestion,
relying on olfaction and gustation, respectively (Glencross et al., 2007;
Ols´en & Lundh, 2016). In this study, tautog were observed to elicit a
strong feeding reaction when fed green crabs which included exiting and
remaining out of their shelters until the food was consumed. Conversely,
fish quickly ate the pellets upon introduction into the tank then receded
back into their shelters. Pellets were observed to absorb water relatively
quickly at which point tautog did not express interest. The primary
source of mortality in the pellet treatments was observed to be starva-
tion. Based on these observations, improving palatability may improve
growth and survival of tautog fed commercial pellets.

Tautog are a stomachless fish (Chao, 1973), and in the wild they have
been observed to forage continuously throughout the day (Olla et al.,
1974, 1975; Steimle & Shaheen, 1999). The prolonged feeding activity
observed in the green crab diet presumably was the cause of the
increased quantity of feed required to elicit satiation. As such, total
caloric intake was significantly higher in the treatments fed green crab
due to the 20-fold increase in feed quantity consumed, despite the
commercial feed being 4 times more calorically dense. Additionally,
while it is unknown in tautog, some fish have been documented to
possess digestive chitinases, which due to the high shell content in the
diet, could have provided an additional energetic benefit (Danulat &
Kausch, 1984; Fines & Holt, 2010).

While the green crab diet was documented to elicit strong feeding
reactions and improved growth, several challenges were encountered
with the raw food diet. Preparation of the diet and the daily removal of
the shell material was labor intensive. The high volume of crab at the
end of the experiment (~11 kg/day) resulted in the leaching of nutrients
into the RAS system which increased microbial activity resulting in a
degradation of water quality. Increased water replacement and daily
filter replacements were required to maintain adequate water quality
through the duration of the experiment. Finally, due to the quantity
required, the cost of the green crab diet was significantly higher than the

commercial pellet diet, if the green crabs were to be purchased as feed in
commercial operations. Each of these issues would challenge the com-
mercial scalability of this feeding methodology. Despite these challenges
this study was able to document high survival and significantly
improved growth rates compared to previous studies. Additional
research is required to develop alternative feeds for non-piscivorous fish
which can optimize growth while being operationally and economically
effective.
